How a frozen protein becomes a picture

Field notes for the model above, from gun to detector to map.

01The column, top to bottom

The field-emission gun pulls electrons from a sharp tip and accelerates them through 300 kV, which gives them a wavelength of about 0.02 Å. Two condenser lenses and the C2 aperture shape them into a nearly parallel beam over the area you want to image.

The beam crosses the grid in the gap of the objective lens, which forms the first and most important image. Its aperture, at the back focal plane, removes wide-angle scattering. The projector lenses magnify that image onto a direct electron detector, which counts single electrons and records each exposure as a movie. Every lens is an electromagnet: a copper coil in an iron yoke. The cutaways show both.

02Why we image out of focus

Protein in ice barely absorbs electrons. It shifts the phase of the electron wave by a few percent, and a perfectly focused image of a phase object has almost no contrast. Defocusing the objective by 0.5–3 µm converts phase shifts into intensity through the contrast transfer function, CTF ≈ sin χ(k), plus about 7% amplitude contrast.

More defocus gives stronger low-resolution contrast, but the CTF oscillates faster and flips sign at each zero. Those zeros are the Thon rings in the power spectrum. Processing software fits them to measure the defocus of every micrograph and corrects for it.

Try it: set defocus to 0 and the particles almost vanish. Push it to 3.5 µm and they stand out while the rings crowd together.

03The grid, from millimetres to nanometres

The grid is a 3 mm copper disc with a 300-mesh lattice of bars (about 85 µm pitch) carrying a holey carbon film; here R1.2/1.3, meaning 1.2 µm holes every 2.5 µm. A few microlitres of protein are blotted to a thin film and plunged into liquid ethane, which freezes the water as a glass, vitreous ice, before crystals can form.

Ice thickness varies across the grid, so a session goes atlas → square → hole → exposure, and data are taken only where the ice is a little thicker than the particle. On this grid, bars block the beam, some squares are dry or torn, thick ice goes dark, and ice is thinnest at the centre of each hole.

Try it: in the Atlas view, drag toward the dark squares, then drop to Exposure and compare the contrast.

04Dose and noise

Electrons break bonds in the protein, so the total exposure is held to about 40–60 e⁻/Å², spread over dozens of movie frames that are aligned to undo beam-induced motion. At the 4 Å pixels used here, 40 e⁻/Å² is about 640 electrons per pixel. Shot noise is then about as large as the protein signal, which is why a single micrograph looks grainy and why the low-pass view helps the eye.

Try it: drop the dose below 10 e⁻/Å². The particles sink into the noise and the Thon rings fade.

05From particles to a map

Each particle is a 2D projection of the same molecule in a random orientation. Software picks hundreds of thousands of them, sorts them by view in 2D classification, estimates their angles and back-projects them into a 3D map. Tilting the stage helps when particles all lie the same way on the grid, at the cost of thicker ice along the beam.

β-galactosidase from E. coli is a D2-symmetric tetramer of 4 × 116 kDa (about 465 kDa, roughly 17 × 13 × 9 nm), and a standard test specimen. Its symmetry gives four copies of the asymmetric unit per particle; a 2015 study reached 2.2 Å with it (Bartesaghi et al., Science).

06What this model simplifies

Particles are built from the deposited structure, PDB 5A1A: its Cα trace averaged into one bead per four residues for the images and one per sixteen for the 3D view, where the four chains are shaded separately. Side chains, solvent and the true scattering potential are left out. Exposures are 102 nm crops simulated with a weak-phase CTF (Cs 2.7 mm, 7% amplitude contrast), shot noise and random ice texture; there is no beam-induced motion, astigmatism or energy filter. The column geometry is schematic, and the in-focus plane is drawn far further from the grid than the real micrometre offset.

Optics: 300 kV (λ = 0.0197 Å), Cs 2.7 mm, B-factor 60 Å². Particle: PDB 5A1A (Bartesaghi et al. 2015), Cα beads. Grid: Cu 300 mesh, R1.2/1.3 holey carbon, ice inelastic mean free path taken as 260 nm.
